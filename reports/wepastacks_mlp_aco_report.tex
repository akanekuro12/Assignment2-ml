\begin{abstract}
This project studies whether recent inbound activity can support warehouse-buffer congestion prediction and proactive transport decisions. The provided WEPAStacks event records are aggregated into 15-minute intervals for four inbound points. A compact multilayer perceptron (MLP) uses current buffer utilisation, current arrivals, a 60-minute rolling arrival rate, and a one-hot dock identifier to estimate whether a simulated no-drone queue is currently at, or will reach, the configured congestion threshold during the next 60 minutes.

The primary routing method is MLP-guided ant colony optimisation (MLP--ACO). It is designed to use the MLP risk score when constructing candidate multi-drone pickup routes subject to payload and return-battery constraints. Route feasibility and policy quality remain evaluation questions rather than established results. The remote warehouses, their capacities and coordinates, the central depot, and the drone fleet are simulated experimental assumptions rather than properties of WEPAStacks. Final predictive and operational results are intentionally withheld until the current-fill baseline, per-dock support, complete held-out routing period, and repeated ACO seeds have been evaluated consistently. The report therefore presents the task, proposed method, evaluation protocol, and known limitations without claiming numerical superiority.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} warehouse congestion, multilayer perceptron, time-series classification, ant colony optimisation, drone routing, WEPAStacks

\section{Introduction}

Inbound congestion occurs when arriving goods accumulate faster than a warehouse handling system can remove them. A candidate decision-support system should identify both current congestion and near-term risk, while its transport policy should translate that information into actions that can be checked against operational constraints. This project investigates a possible link between these two tasks. Under the current label definition, the MLP is intended to estimate whether a dock is already at, or will reach, the congestion threshold within one hour. A routing layer is then intended to use that risk score when selecting simulated satellite warehouses and candidate drone routes.

The practical motivation is that a purely reactive policy waits until the buffer is already close to full. If congestion can be forecast from the current queue and recent arrivals, a vehicle may be dispatched earlier. A current-fill rule ignores whether arrivals are increasing and whether different docks have different patterns. This motivates testing a learning model; it does not by itself prove that machine learning is better. The project must compare the risk-aware policy with current-fill and greedy baselines. A small MLP is selected as a lower-complexity candidate because the input is tabular and the recent 60-minute history is already summarised. Its interpretability and deployment suitability still require assessment.

The central research question is:

\begin{quote}
On the chronological held-out period, does MLP-guided ACO reduce total overflow compared with reactive routing policies, while satisfying every payload and return-battery constraint and reporting the resulting energy cost?
\end{quote}

This question contains a prediction stage and an operational stage. The prediction stage asks whether seven present-and-past features can identify current or next-hour threshold status under a no-drone counterfactual. The operational stage asks whether using that signal improves ACO decisions.

\section{Task Definition}

\subsection{Primary contribution and project boundary}

The proposed contribution is an MLP--ACO pipeline. The MLP is intended to estimate current-or-next-hour threshold risk, and ACO is intended to use that signal when prioritising candidate pickups. Nearest-first, fullest-first, and ACO using only current fill are the planned main baselines.

The report uses the drone-only queue definition as the main task. Under this simulation rule, goods leave a satellite warehouse only when a simulated drone collects them. The earlier service-rate model with capacity 20 is treated as a preliminary experiment and is not used as evidence for the final MLP--ACO claim. Before final evaluation, the main model must be trained and checked using the same capacities, fleet assumptions, historical queue policy, and queue semantics as deployment.

\subsection{Training interface}

Each training observation represents one dock at the end of a 15-minute interval. The input vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{i,t} = [r_{i,t},\; a_{i,t},\; \bar{a}^{(60)}_{i,t},\; d_{i,1},\; d_{i,2},\; d_{i,3},\; d_{i,4}] \in \R^7,
\end{equation}
where $r_{i,t}$ is the buffer fill ratio, $a_{i,t}$ is the current number of arrivals, $\bar{a}^{(60)}_{i,t}$ is the mean arrival count over the current and previous three intervals, and $d_{i,j}$ is a one-hot dock indicator. The feature definition is intended to restrict input values to information available at or before time $t$; this property must be confirmed in the final leakage audit.

The binary training target assumes that no drone will collect goods during the next four intervals. It is
\begin{equation}
y_{i,t}=\mathbb{1}\!\left[\max_{h\in\{1,2,3,4\}}
\left(A_{i,t}+\sum_{s=1}^{h}a_{i,t+s}\right) \geq 0.8K_i\right],
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $A_{i,t}$ is the available stock before the current pickup decision and $K_i$ is warehouse capacity. The configured capacities are 200 units for Warehouse~1 and 80 units for Warehouses~2--4. Future arrivals are required for historical label construction. The feature-building procedure is intended to exclude them from the input vector, and that separation must be verified before final reporting.

\subsection{Expected behaviour}

Given a low fill ratio and low recent arrivals, the MLP should produce a low risk score. A high or rapidly increasing queue should raise the score. Because the current target includes stock already at the congestion threshold, final evaluation must distinguish current-state detection from genuine below-threshold forecasting. In routing deployment, a high-risk warehouse may become active before it is physically full. Every proposed route must begin and end at the central depot, stay within payload and available-battery limits, collect only from visited warehouses, and never collect more than the requested demand.

Two thresholds have different purposes. A validation-selected classification threshold, $\theta_{\mathrm{class}}$, converts scores into labels for reporting MLP metrics. A separately selected routing threshold, $\theta_{\mathrm{route}}$, decides whether a warehouse becomes active early in the operational simulator. These values are not interchangeable. Both thresholds remain to be finalised using validation data: the classification threshold will be selected from predictive metrics, whereas the routing threshold will be tuned against overflow and energy in validation simulations. Neither threshold will be selected on the final test period.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}

\subsection{Dataset structure and quality}

The current cleaned dataset contains 205,258 inbound events and nine fields: arrival time, relative day, hour, minute, 15-minute time bin, dock, SKU, batch, and week. It covers relative days 2--89. Preliminary checks recorded by the current preprocessing workflow did not identify missing values, invalid dock identifiers, negative arrival times, or fully duplicated rows. The workflow also reported agreement between the derived day, hour, minute, and time-bin fields and arrival time. These checks should be rerun and independently reviewed before submission. There are 4,763 repeated arrival-time and dock combinations; they are currently retained because multiple distinct cargo events may occur at the same dock in the same second.

An evident data-quality concern is representativeness. Dock~1 contains 93.26\% of all events, while Docks~2--4 disappear early in the timeline. In the current cleaned file, events after day~39 are assigned only to Dock~1. The reason for this pattern is not established by the dataset and should not be inferred without additional metadata.

\subsection{Aggregation and zero intervals}

Arrival time is divided by 900 seconds and rounded down to obtain a 15-minute time bin. Events are grouped by time bin and dock. A complete Cartesian grid is then created from all 8,395 consecutive time bins and the four docks, producing 33,580 dock--interval rows before rolling and future-horizon exclusions. Missing counts are filled with zero. This step is necessary because a missing event row may mean that an active dock received no goods during the interval.

The same operation also creates a limitation. For Docks~2--4, a zero after the final recorded event may mean either zero demand or missing/inactive coverage. The dataset has no \texttt{dock\_active} field, so the pipeline cannot distinguish these cases. The report therefore treats late zero rows for Docks~2--4 cautiously.

\subsection{Rolling features, queue simulation, and leakage control}

The 60-minute rolling feature is the mean of arrivals in the current and previous three intervals. The first three rows for each dock are discarded because a complete window is unavailable. Historical training states are generated chronologically by a fixed fullest-first drone policy. The policy is designed to read current available goods, current battery, capacities, and distances without using future arrivals or MLP predictions. This restriction remains part of the final leakage audit. The state update is
\begin{align}
A_{i,t} &= Q_{i,t}+a_{i,t},\\
R_{i,t} &= \max(0,A_{i,t}-P_{i,t}),\\
O_{i,t} &= \max(0,R_{i,t}-K_i),\\
Q_{i,t+1} &= \min(K_i,R_{i,t}),
\end{align}
where $P_{i,t}$ is the quantity collected by drones. There is no hidden service rate. Battery is reset at the start of each simulated day. The feature \texttt{buffer\_fill\_ratio} is calculated from $A_{i,t}$ before pickup, so it can exceed 1 when arrivals temporarily exceed capacity; its observed modelling range is 0.1625 to 1.55.

The final four rows for each dock are discarded because they do not have a complete future label horizon. The resulting modelling table contains 33,552 observations. The pipeline attempts to reduce leakage risk by using current and past arrivals as features, using a fixed non-ML historical policy, fitting the scaler and class weights on training data, inserting one-hour gaps at split boundaries, choosing the classification threshold on validation data, and reserving test data for final evaluation. These design choices reduce obvious leakage paths but do not replace an independent audit of feature timestamps, state updates, and split boundaries.

\subsection{MLP architecture}

The network has two hidden layers. The first maps seven inputs to 32 ReLU units, and the second maps 32 values to 16 ReLU units. A single sigmoid output produces a risk score. Glorot uniform initialisation is used for the dense-layer weights, and all biases start at zero. No dropout layer is used in the drone-compatible implementation.

The forward pass can be written as
\begin{align}
\mathbf{h}_1 &= \operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_1),\\
\mathbf{h}_2 &= \operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{h}_1+\mathbf{b}_2),\\
\hat{p} &= \sigma(\mathbf{w}_3^\top\mathbf{h}_2+b_3).
\end{align}

This structure is relatively small and permits nonlinear interactions. ReLU is selected to reduce the saturation concerns associated with sigmoid hidden units, while a sigmoid output is conventional for binary classification. These architectural motivations follow standard feed-forward neural-network practice \cite{rumelhart1986,glorot2010}; they do not by themselves establish that the architecture is suitable for this dataset.

\subsection{Loss, optimiser, and class imbalance}

The network minimises weighted binary cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{y_n}
\left[y_n\log \hat{p}_n+(1-y_n)\log(1-\hat{p}_n)\right].
\label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}
Balanced weights computed from the 22,628 training labels are $w_0=0.9024$ and $w_1=1.1212$. These weights are intended to compensate for the 44.60\% positive-class rate, but they do not resolve unequal temporal coverage across docks. Adam is configured with learning rate $10^{-3}$, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, and $\epsilon=10^{-7}$ \cite{kingma2015}. The planned training procedure uses mini-batches of 256, at most 100 epochs, and early stopping on validation loss with patience eight and restoration of the best weights.

\subsection{Risk-aware ACO routing}

The drone study maps the four inbound points to four simulated satellite warehouses and adds a central depot. At interval $t$, available goods are the previous queue plus current arrivals. A warehouse is active when it contains goods and either its predicted risk exceeds a risk threshold or its current fill ratio exceeds the fill threshold. Urgency combines predicted risk and current fill:
\begin{equation}
u_{i,t}=0.60\,\hat{p}_{i,t}+0.40\min(1,r_{i,t}).
\end{equation}

An ant chooses a candidate next warehouse from the set currently considered feasible by the routing checks, using pheromone $\tau_{ij}$ and heuristic desirability $\eta_{ij}$:
\begin{equation}
P(i\rightarrow j)=\frac{\tau_{ij}^{\alpha}\eta_{ij}^{\beta}}
{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{F}}\tau_{ik}^{\alpha}\eta_{ik}^{\beta}},
\qquad
\eta_{ij}=\frac{\varepsilon+u_j}{\varepsilon+d_{ij}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ is intended to contain only warehouses that pass the current payload and estimated return-energy checks. The implementation uses $\alpha=1$, $\beta=2$, 30 ants, at most 100 iterations, evaporation 0.10, and a stall limit of 20 iterations. Pheromone is clipped to a fixed numerical range. This follows the constructive and iterative logic of ant-colony routing \cite{dorigo1997}; final feasibility must still be assessed independently from the optimiser.

The ACO objective is
\begin{equation}
J=0.30\frac{E_{\mathrm{used}}}{\sum_d B_d+\varepsilon}
+0.70\frac{\sum_i u_iR_i}{\sum_i u_iD_i+\varepsilon},
\label{eq:aco-objective}
\end{equation}
where $D_i$ is active demand and $R_i$ is remaining demand after pickup. The constraints are
\begin{align}
\sum_i x_{d,i} &\leq C_d^{\mathrm{payload}},\\
E(\text{route}_d) &\leq B_d^{\mathrm{available}},\\
x_{d,i} &>0 \Rightarrow i\in\text{route}_d,\\
\sum_d x_{d,i} &\leq D_i.
\end{align}
The route representation requires each route to start and end at node 0. The candidate-selection battery calculation is intended to include the energy required to return from a warehouse, rather than checking only the next leg. Independent route validation remains necessary because this is an implementation requirement, not evidence that every generated route is feasible.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

Accuracy alone is insufficient when class balance and temporal coverage differ across docks. Precision measures how many warnings are correct. Recall measures how many threshold cases are detected. F1 balances precision and recall. Balanced accuracy averages sensitivity and specificity. Average precision summarises the precision--recall curve \cite{saito2015}. The confusion matrix and per-dock support are essential because strong global performance can still reflect a dock-identity shortcut rather than general temporal prediction.

For routing, the metrics are total overflow, total collected goods, energy, distance, number of sorties, remaining active demand, runtime, and constraint violations. The primary comparison is MLP--ACO against nearest-first, fullest-first, and ACO using only current fill. A complete result requires the entire held-out period and repeated ACO seeds. Preliminary or partial runs are used only for implementation debugging and are not reported as evidence.

\section{Results Pending Final Evaluation}

Numerical MLP and routing results are intentionally omitted from this draft. Earlier development runs were useful for checking that the pipeline executed, but they do not yet satisfy the evaluation protocol defined in Table~\ref{tab:success-criteria}. In particular, the final report must not treat a partial routing run, a single ACO seed, or a classifier result without a current-fill baseline as evidence of superiority.

The completed version of this section will report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item validation and test precision, recall, F1, PR--AUC, ROC--AUC, Brier score, and confusion matrices at a threshold selected only on validation data;
  \item an always-negative baseline, a current-fill baseline, and an ablation without dock one-hot features;
  \item class support and predictive metrics separately for each dock;
  \item full held-out routing results for nearest-first, fullest-first, ACO-current, and MLP--ACO using identical arrival streams and initial states;
  \item repeated-seed summaries, including means, standard deviations, confidence intervals, runtime, and constraint violations; and
  \item route-level diagnostics explaining any operational difference between reactive and risk-aware policies.
\end{itemize}

Until these experiments are completed, this report makes no numerical claim that the MLP generalises to unseen congestion patterns or that MLP guidance improves ACO routing.

\subsection{Implementation verification}

The codebase contains ACO, greedy baselines, a small-instance reference solver, a chronological rolling simulator, metric utilities, and a separate solution validator. The test suite includes cases concerning full-grid aggregation, simultaneous events, label boundaries, future-information exclusion, drone-only queue updates, fixed-seed ACO behaviour, payload and return-battery limits, demand limits, and agreement between route legs and energy calculations. Their final pass status and coverage must be recorded before submission. Even passing these tests would provide evidence only for the tested cases; it would not establish complete implementation correctness or predictive and operational superiority.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Answer to the research question}

The research question cannot yet be answered because the required final comparison has not been completed. The prediction stage must first be compared with simple baselines and examined separately for each dock. The routing stage must then be evaluated over the complete held-out period with repeated ACO seeds. Until both stages are complete, the report does not claim that MLP--ACO reduces overflow or improves resource use.

\subsection{Training loss versus the practical objective}

Binary cross-entropy and the operational objective answer different questions. BCE rewards separation of the simulated binary label. Warehouse operation cares about overflow, the timing and size of pickups, remaining battery, energy, and uncollected goods. Consequently, strong classifier metrics will not be treated as evidence of a useful dispatch policy.

The classification threshold and routing threshold will be selected separately. The former will be chosen from validation classification performance, whereas the latter will be selected through validation simulation using overflow and energy. A stronger future method could add a minimum-load rule, include remaining daily battery in the activation decision, or train a decision-aware model whose loss approximates downstream cost.

\subsection{Implications}

Prediction and decision quality must be evaluated separately. The chosen MLP is relatively compact, but its actual training cost, inference cost, and deployment suitability have not yet been quantified. Classifier performance alone cannot establish that its scores produce a useful dispatch policy. Chronological splitting reduces some direct temporal leakage risks, yet it does not by itself correct unequal temporal coverage across docks.

Similarly, a feasible route is not necessarily an operationally effective route. The deployment claim must be based on overflow, collection, energy, and runtime under a common evaluation stream, rather than on feasibility or classification accuracy alone.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Simulated target.} Queue length and congestion are generated from assumed capacities, drone behaviour, and a no-drone future scenario. The model predicts the simulator, not directly observed warehouse congestion.
  \item \textbf{Dock coverage shift.} Docks~2--4 end early in the available event stream. Their validation and test class support must be inspected before any positive-class metric is interpreted.
  \item \textbf{Ambiguous zero intervals.} Missing or inactive coverage is encoded in the same way as an active dock with zero arrivals.
  \item \textbf{Dominant Dock 1.} Dock~1 contains 93.26\% of inbound events, so the fitted shared model may be strongly influenced by its operating pattern.
  \item \textbf{Potential dock-label confounding.} Dock one-hot features may act as a shortcut if class support differs strongly across docks; this must be quantified in the final per-dock evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Uncalibrated score.} Weighted BCE and F1-based threshold selection can produce useful ranking without producing a calibrated probability.
  \item \textbf{Limited feature set.} The baseline excludes hour-of-day, shift, robot state, downtime, service completions, and SKU mixture.
  \item \textbf{Simplified drone physics.} The routing model omits weather, no-fly zones, collision avoidance, three-dimensional trajectories, charging schedules, and time windows.
  \item \textbf{Incomplete routing evaluation.} Partial development runs cannot estimate stochastic variation or full-period performance and are excluded from the reported evidence.
  \item \textbf{Metric comparability.} Risk-weighted remaining goods may not be comparable when some policies log risk as zero. A common exogenous risk series would be needed before using this metric in policy comparisons.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommended Refinements}

One important improvement is to obtain data that distinguish an active dock with no arrivals from an inactive or unobserved dock. A \texttt{dock\_active} field, operating calendar, and documented reason for the early termination of Docks~2--4 would support a more defensible temporal grid. Real queue length, service completions, incident logs, and confirmed capacities could then be used to replace or assess Eq.~\eqref{eq:target} against an observed target.

Model evaluation should preserve chronological splits and report support for every metric. The first ablation should remove dock one-hot inputs; a second should test a simple current-fill or logistic-regression predictor. Probability calibration should be assessed on validation data using a reliability diagram and Brier score. After these changes, a new untouched test period should be reserved.

The routing threshold must be selected with validation simulations rather than classification F1. Candidate policies should also test a minimum pickup load or a reserve of daily battery for later intervals. These mechanisms address the possible risk that premature small pickups reduce resources available for later demand peaks.

The final routing experiment should run nearest-first, fullest-first, ACO-current, and MLP--ACO on identical copies of the complete held-out stream. ACO should use all ten configured seeds. Results should include mean, standard deviation, and confidence intervals for overflow, goods collected, energy, distance, remaining demand, sorties, and runtime. Small active instances should be compared with the reference solver; any claim that it provides an exact optimum must first be verified against its formulation and implementation.

\section{Conclusion}

This report proposes a compact MLP--ACO framework for simulated warehouse-congestion prediction and drone routing. The MLP feature design is intended to use only information available at decision time, while the ACO design includes payload, demand, and return-battery constraints. Both properties require final audit and empirical verification. The report explicitly distinguishes the provided WEPAStacks arrival records from simulated queues, capacities, layout, and drone-fleet assumptions.

Numerical performance claims are withheld from this draft. The final conclusion will be written only after the MLP has been compared with simple prediction baselines, class support has been examined by dock, routing thresholds have been selected on validation simulations, and every routing policy has been run on the complete held-out stream with repeated seeds. Until then, the report documents a proposed implementation and evaluation framework; it does not claim predictive accuracy, complete correctness, route feasibility, deployment readiness, or operational superiority.

\section{Metric Definitions}

For true positives $TP$, false positives $FP$, true negatives $TN$, and false negatives $FN$:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, &
\mathrm{Recall} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FN},\\
\mathrm{Specificity} &= \frac{TN}{TN+FP}, &
\mathrm{F1} &= \frac{2\,\mathrm{Precision}\,\mathrm{Recall}}
{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}},\\
\mathrm{Balanced\ Accuracy} &= \frac{\mathrm{Recall}+\mathrm{Specificity}}{2}.
\end{align}
The Brier score is the mean squared difference between the predicted score and the binary target. It is included as a preliminary diagnostic, but a reliability plot is still required before the sigmoid output can be interpreted as a calibrated probability.
