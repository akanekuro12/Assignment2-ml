\begin{abstract}
This project studies congestion prediction and drone pickup decisions using the WEPAStacks inbound-delivery data. Events from four inbound points are grouped into 15-minute intervals. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) uses seven current and recent features to produce one risk score for each warehouse. The target is positive when the warehouse is expected to reach 80\% capacity within the next hour, assuming that no pickup occurs during that period.

Warehouses with goods are passed to ant colony optimisation (ACO) when their MLP score is at least 0.70. ACO then constructs a route for one simulated drone using pheromone and distance. On the held-out data, the MLP achieved an F1 score of 0.9699, compared with 0.8769 for the current-fill baseline. MLP--ACO reduced simulated overflow by 11.38\% relative to ACO-current, but required more distance and energy. These results have two important limits: all positive validation and test labels belong to Warehouse~1, and no evaluated interval contains more than one active warehouse. The experiment therefore evaluates prediction and pickup timing, but not the quality of multi-stop ACO routing.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} warehouse congestion, multilayer perceptron, time-series classification, ant colony optimisation, drone routing, WEPAStacks

\section{Introduction}

Inbound congestion occurs when goods arrive faster than they can be removed. A useful system should warn about current or near-term congestion and choose pickups that satisfy distance, energy, and payload limits. This project studies both tasks. The MLP estimates congestion risk for the current state and the next hour. ACO receives only the warehouses that pass the MLP filter and constructs a route for one drone.

A current-fill rule uses only the goods that are already waiting. It does not use recent arrival patterns and may therefore react late. The MLP is tested to determine whether recent arrivals provide useful warning information. It is compared with an always-negative rule and a current-fill rule. The routing policy is compared with nearest-first, fullest-first, and ACO-current. The MLP is deliberately small because the input is a short tabular feature vector.

The central research question is:

\begin{quote}
On the chronological held-out period, does filtering warehouses with an MLP risk threshold before single-drone ACO reduce overflow relative to current-fill routing, while respecting route-distance, energy, and payload constraints?
\end{quote}

The prediction stage asks whether seven present-and-past features improve threshold classification over simple rules. The operational stage asks whether the resulting filter improves routing outcomes. A high classification score alone cannot answer the operational question.

\section{Part A --- Task Definition}

\subsection{Objective and Motivation}

The proposed contribution is an MLP-filtered ACO pipeline for one drone. The MLP produces one score per warehouse. A hard threshold of 0.70 determines which non-empty warehouses are visible to ACO. The score is not reused as an urgency weight, projected-stock estimate, or ACO objective term. This separation makes the role of machine learning testable: it changes the candidate set, while ACO remains a distance-based route optimiser.

The report uses a drone-only queue definition. Goods leave a simulated warehouse only when the single drone collects them. Battery is restored at the beginning of every 15-minute interval, representing replacement or charging at the depot. The earlier service-rate and multi-drone designs are outside the scope of the current experiment. The training-state simulator and deployment simulator use the same warehouse capacities, single-drone configuration, per-interval battery policy, and queue-update equations.

\subsection{Project Scope and Data Assumptions}

The project distinguishes fields in the provided WEPAStacks records from experimental assumptions introduced by the simulation. The supplied fields describe a temporal stream of inbound events; the current file does not include four geographically separated warehouses, drone specifications, a central depot, or measured congestion labels.

\subsection{Training and Deployment I/O}

Each training observation represents one dock at the end of a 15-minute interval. The input vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{i,t} = [r_{i,t},\; a_{i,t},\; \bar{a}^{(60)}_{i,t},\; d_{i,1},\; d_{i,2},\; d_{i,3},\; d_{i,4}] \in \R^7,
\end{equation}
where $r_{i,t}$ is the buffer fill ratio, $a_{i,t}$ is the current number of arrivals, $\bar{a}^{(60)}_{i,t}$ is the mean arrival count over the current and previous three intervals, and $d_{i,j}$ is a one-hot dock indicator. The feature definition is intended to restrict input values to information available at or before time $t$; this property must be confirmed in the final leakage audit.

The binary training target assumes that no drone will collect goods during the next four intervals. It is
\begin{equation}
y_{i,t}=\mathbb{1}\!\left[\max_{h\in\{1,2,3,4\}}
\left(A_{i,t}+\sum_{s=1}^{h}a_{i,t+s}\right) \geq 0.8K_i\right],
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $A_{i,t}$ is the available stock before the current pickup decision and $K_i$ is warehouse capacity. The configured capacities are 200 units for Warehouse~1 and 80 units for Warehouses~2--4. Future arrivals are required for historical label construction. The feature-building procedure is intended to exclude them from the input vector, and that separation must be verified before final reporting.

\section{Part B --- Model, Algorithm, and System Structure}

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}

\subsubsection{Dataset Structure and Quality}

The supplied WEPAStacks documentation describes 89 days of operations in a finished-goods warehouse and three source files: a layout file, an order-event file, and an initial-fill file. The current implementation does not consume all three files directly. It uses a cleaned CSV derived from delivery events at inbound points 1--4; retrieval events, outbound points, the original layout, and initial inventory are outside the present learning interface.

The cleaned dataset contains 205,258 inbound events from days 2--89. Its nine fields include arrival time, dock, SKU, batch, and several derived time fields. The preprocessing checks found no missing values, invalid dock identifiers, negative times, or fully duplicated rows. There are 4,763 repeated combinations of arrival time and dock. They are retained because different cargo events can arrive at the same dock in the same second.

The main data-quality concern is unequal dock coverage. Dock~1 contains 93.26\% of all events, and Docks~2--4 disappear early in the timeline. After day~39, the file contains events only for Dock~1. The dataset does not explain why this happens.

\subsubsection{Aggregation and Zero Intervals}

Arrival time is divided into 15-minute intervals, and events are counted for each dock. The pipeline then creates one row for every dock in every interval. This produces 33,580 dock--interval rows before incomplete rolling windows are removed. Missing event counts are filled with zero because an interval with no row may simply mean that no goods arrived.

This step also creates uncertainty. For Docks~2--4, a zero after the final recorded event may mean either no arrivals or that the dock was no longer recorded. The dataset has no \texttt{dock\_active} field, so these cases cannot be separated.

\subsubsection{Rolling Features, Queue Simulation, and Leakage Control}

The 60-minute feature is the mean arrival count across the current and previous three intervals. The first three rows for each dock are removed because they do not have a complete window. A fixed fullest-first drone policy generates the historical queue states in time order. It uses only the information available at the current interval and does not use future arrivals or MLP predictions. The queue update is
\begin{align}
A_{i,t} &= Q_{i,t}+a_{i,t},\\
R_{i,t} &= \max(0,A_{i,t}-P_{i,t}),\\
O_{i,t} &= \max(0,R_{i,t}-K_i),\\
Q_{i,t+1} &= \min(K_i,R_{i,t}),
\end{align}
where $P_{i,t}$ is the quantity collected by the single drone. There is no separate service-rate term. Battery is reset before each simulated interval. The feature \texttt{buffer\_fill\_ratio} is calculated from $A_{i,t}$ before pickup, so it can exceed 1 when arrivals temporarily exceed capacity; its current modelling range is 0.20 to 1.55.

The final four rows for each dock are removed because their one-hour future target is incomplete. The final modelling table contains 33,552 observations. To reduce time leakage, the features use only current and past information, the scaler and class weights are fitted on the training set, and one-hour gaps are placed between data splits. Validation data is used during model development, while test data is reserved for final evaluation.

\subsection{MLP architecture}

The network has two hidden layers. The first maps seven inputs to 32 ReLU units, and the second maps 32 values to 16 ReLU units. A single sigmoid output produces a risk score. Glorot uniform initialisation is used for the dense-layer weights, and all biases start at zero. No dropout layer is used in the drone-compatible implementation.

The forward pass can be written as
\begin{align}
\mathbf{h}_1 &= \operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_1),\\
\mathbf{h}_2 &= \operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{h}_1+\mathbf{b}_2),\\
\hat{p} &= \sigma(\mathbf{w}_3^\top\mathbf{h}_2+b_3).
\end{align}

The two hidden layers allow the model to learn nonlinear relationships between current fill and recent arrivals. The complete network has only 801 trainable parameters, so it is a lightweight model for this tabular dataset. ReLU is used in the hidden layers, and the sigmoid output maps the final value to a score between zero and one \cite{rumelhart1986,glorot2010}.

\subsection{Loss, optimiser, and class imbalance}

The network minimises weighted binary cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{y_n}
\left[y_n\log \hat{p}_n+(1-y_n)\log(1-\hat{p}_n)\right].
\label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}
The class weights are $w_0=0.6925$ and $w_1=1.7984$. They make mistakes on the less common positive class more costly during training. This helps with class imbalance but cannot fix the unequal data coverage between warehouses. Binary cross-entropy is used because the target is binary and the model has a sigmoid output. However, this training loss does not directly measure overflow, distance, or energy.

For each mini-batch, Keras calculates the forward pass and the weighted loss in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bce}. TensorFlow then calculates the gradients automatically, and the optimiser uses them to update the network weights. This means that backpropagation follows the stated loss even though the gradients are not coded by hand.

Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$ was compared with SGD at learning rate $10^{-2}$ using the same 32--16 network, data split, and three seeds. Table~\ref{tab:optimizer-comparison} shows that Adam obtained the higher mean validation PR--AUC and F1 and reached its best validation loss earlier. SGD reached the 100-epoch limit in all three runs, which suggests that this SGD setting would require further learning-rate or momentum tuning. Adam was therefore retained for the final model \cite{kingma2015}. This is a comparison of two specified settings, not proof that Adam is the best possible optimiser.

\subsection{Framework and implementation choices}

pandas and NumPy are used for data cleaning, 15-minute aggregation, queue updates, and array calculations. scikit-learn provides feature scaling and evaluation metrics. TensorFlow/Keras provides the MLP layers, training process, early stopping, and model saving. During deployment, the saved scaler and feature order are loaded with the model so that training and inference use the same inputs.

ACO is implemented directly in Python so that its transition rule and route constraints are visible in the code. Route validation is kept separate from route construction. The validator recalculates payload, distance, energy, and warehouse eligibility instead of trusting the optimiser's output. For only four warehouses, testing every possible route is also practical and provides a useful reference for ACO.

\subsection{MLP filter and single-drone ACO}

At interval $t$, available goods are $A_{i,t}=Q_{i,t}+a_{i,t}$. For MLP--ACO, the active set is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_t=\{i\mid A_{i,t}>0\ \land\ \hat p_{i,t}\geq0.70\}.
\label{eq:active-filter}
\end{equation}
Warehouses outside $\mathcal{S}_t$ are absent from the ACO input and cannot appear in its route. For nearest-first, fullest-first, and ACO-current, the active set instead uses the current-fill rule $A_{i,t}/K_i\geq0.80$. This is the only difference between ACO-current and MLP--ACO; both use the same route optimiser.

Each ant begins at depot 0 and maintains the travelled distance. A candidate warehouse $j$ is feasible only if the route can visit it and still return to the depot:
\begin{align}
D_{\mathrm{proj}} &= D_{\mathrm{so\ far}}+d_{ij}+d_{j0},\\
D_{\mathrm{proj}} &\leq D_{\max},\\
D_{\mathrm{proj}}e &\leq B_{\mathrm{available}},
\end{align}
where $e$ is energy per kilometre. Among feasible unvisited warehouses, the transition probability is
\begin{equation}
P(i\rightarrow j)=
\frac{\tau_{ij}^{\alpha}\left(1/(\varepsilon+d_{ij})\right)^{\beta}}
{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{F}}\tau_{ik}^{\alpha}\left(1/(\varepsilon+d_{ik})\right)^{\beta}}.
\label{eq:aco-transition}
\end{equation}
The MLP score and amount of goods do not appear in this transition rule. The implementation uses $\alpha=1$, $\beta=2$, 30 ants, at most 100 iterations, evaporation 0.10, and a 20-iteration stall limit.

For route $R$, the objective is
\begin{equation}
J(R)=1000\,|\mathcal{S}_t\setminus V(R)|+D(R),
\label{eq:aco-objective}
\end{equation}
where $V(R)$ is the set of visited active warehouses. Since every feasible route is limited to 30~km, the penalty makes an additional active visit more important than any possible distance saving. Among routes visiting the same number of active warehouses, the shorter route wins. After each iteration, pheromone evaporates and the global-best route receives an increment proportional to $1/(1+J)$.

After route construction, the 30-unit payload is divided in proportion to available goods at visited warehouses. If $C=\min(30,\sum_{j\in V(R)}A_{j,t})$, then
\begin{equation}
x_{i,t}=C\frac{A_{i,t}}{\sum_{j\in V(R)}A_{j,t}},\qquad i\in V(R).
\end{equation}
The validator recomputes depot endpoints, repeated and filtered nodes, total distance, route energy, payload, and per-warehouse pickup. Goods not collected remain in the queue. Battery is logged after the route and restored before the next interval.

\subsubsection{Deployment pseudocode}

\textbf{Algorithm 1: MLP-filtered routing for one interval}\par
\textit{Input:} current queues, arrivals, saved scaler and MLP, drone and ACO settings.\par
\textit{Output:} a validated route, pickups, updated queues, and interval metrics.

The procedure is:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Add the current arrivals to each warehouse queue.
  \item Build the seven MLP features for all four warehouses.
  \item Apply the saved scaler and MLP to obtain four risk scores.
  \item Keep non-empty warehouses whose scores are at least 0.70.
  \item Use ACO to construct feasible routes over the retained warehouses.
  \item Select the route with the lowest unvisited-node penalty and distance.
  \item Divide the payload in proportion to the goods at visited warehouses.
  \item Validate the route, update the queues, and record overflow, distance, and energy.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure matches the implementation order in \code{run_simulation()}. In particular, the MLP score is used for filtering and is not reused inside the ACO transition probability.

\subsubsection{Computational size and feasibility}

One MLP forward pass uses about $7\times32+32\times16+16\times1=752$ weight multiplications per warehouse, which is small for four warehouses. With $m$ active warehouses, ACO performs approximately $O(IAm^2)$ candidate checks, where $I$ is the number of iterations and $A$ is the number of ants. Exact search is practical for the current four-warehouse experiment, whereas ACO is intended as a method that can be extended to larger routing problems.

\subsection{Implementation Details and Observed Challenges}

\paragraph{Preventing time leakage.}
Future arrivals are needed to create the target, but they must not appear in the seven input features. The data is therefore sorted by warehouse and time, rows with an incomplete future horizon are removed, and the scaler is fitted only on training rows. A chronological split is used because a random split could place later operating states in the training set.

\paragraph{Unequal warehouse coverage.}
Class weighting helps with the overall number of positive and negative labels, but it cannot create missing examples. Warehouse~1 dominates the data, while Warehouses~2--4 have no positive validation or test cases. The report therefore includes results by warehouse, a warehouse-ID ablation, and a logistic-regression baseline.

\paragraph{Hard decision threshold.}
Scores of 0.699 and 0.700 are almost equal, but only the second score passes the routing filter. Weighted BCE also trains the classifier without directly considering overflow, distance, or energy. The threshold experiment is therefore used to show trade-offs, not to claim that 0.70 is optimal. The output is called a risk score rather than an exact probability because its calibration is not perfect.

\paragraph{Limited ACO test cases.}
ACO can construct a route with several stops, but the WEPAStacks evaluation data never has more than one active warehouse in an interval. The synthetic benchmark shows that the implementation matches exact search on the 15 tested small cases. However, these artificial cases do not replace operational evidence with real simultaneous demand.

\section{Part C --- Model Evaluation and Refinement}

\subsection{Expected Model Behaviour}

The MLP should give a low score when current fill and recent arrivals are low. It should give a higher score when the warehouse is close to the congestion limit and arrivals remain high. Ideally, positive cases receive scores near one and negative cases receive scores near zero. Weighted BCE measures this behaviour during training, while held-out classification metrics evaluate it after training. Because some positive cases are already at 80\% capacity, the final metrics include both current congestion detection and early forecasting.

The value 0.80 is used only to create the congestion label from warehouse capacity. The MLP produces a continuous score between zero and one. During deployment, a non-empty warehouse passes the filter when its score is at least 0.70. The best validation threshold is reported for comparison but does not replace the configured routing threshold.

The routing output should start and end at the depot and visit only warehouses that pass the filter. Each warehouse can be visited at most once. The route must remain within 30~km and 30 energy units, and the total pickup cannot exceed 30 cargo units. Prediction metrics evaluate the MLP, while overflow, collection, distance, energy, and constraint checks evaluate the routing policy.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Metrics}

Accuracy alone can be misleading when the classes are imbalanced. Precision measures how many warnings are correct, recall measures how many positive cases are detected, and F1 balances both values. Balanced accuracy gives equal importance to the positive and negative classes. PR--AUC and ROC--AUC measure ranking performance across thresholds \cite{saito2015}. The confusion matrix and per-warehouse sample counts are also reported because a strong overall score may hide weak coverage at some warehouses.

Routing is evaluated using overflow, collected goods, distance, energy, number of sorties, active-warehouse visits, runtime, and constraint violations. MLP--ACO is compared with nearest-first, fullest-first, and ACO-current on the same held-out arrival stream. Each ACO policy is run with ten seeds.

\subsection{Implementation Verification}

The test suite completed 22 tests with no failures. The tests cover the 0.70 boundary, empty and filtered warehouses, depot endpoints, repeated nodes, payload, energy, distance, battery reset, queue updates, fixed seeds, one exact-solver comparison, and reproducible generation of the synthetic routing cases. \subsection{Answer to the Research Question}

For Warehouse~1, the MLP improves F1 from 0.8769 for the current-fill baseline to 0.9699. MLP filtering also reduces simulated overflow by 11.38\% relative to ACO-current. This supports the use of recent arrival information for earlier pickup decisions. The synthetic benchmark supports the multi-stop ACO implementation on small artificial cases, but the WEPAStacks result still does not show positive-case performance at Warehouses~2--4 or operational multi-stop routing.

\subsection{Loss--Objective Mismatch}

Weighted BCE measures prediction error, while the practical system also depends on overflow, distance, and energy. A score change has no routing effect if it remains below 0.70, whereas a change from 0.699 to 0.700 activates a warehouse. Similarly, the ACO objective rewards active visits and short routes but does not directly minimise overflow. For this reason, both prediction and operational metrics are reported.

\subsection{Interpretation and Trade-offs}

The fixed routing threshold is 0.70, while the best validation F1 occurred at 0.9313. Lower thresholds reduce overflow but increase distance and energy. The value 0.70 is therefore a configured operating choice, not a proven best threshold. The sigmoid output is called a risk score because it is not perfectly calibrated as a probability.

With only four warehouses, exact search is practical and provides a useful reference for ACO.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Simulated target.} Queue length and congestion are created from assumed capacities and drone behaviour, not measured warehouse congestion. Some positive cases are already at the 80\% boundary, so the task mixes current detection with future forecasting.
  \item \textbf{Unequal warehouse data.} Warehouse~1 contains 93.26\% of inbound events. Warehouses~2--4 end early and have no positive validation or test labels.
  \item \textbf{Data coverage and score interpretation.} For Warehouses~2--4, a zero may mean no arrivals or missing operation. Removing warehouse ID also changed the metrics, so the output is treated as a risk score rather than an exact probability.
  \item \textbf{Limited feature set.} The baseline excludes hour-of-day, shift, robot state, downtime, service completions, and SKU mixture.
  \item \textbf{Simplified drone model.} Battery is restored every 15 minutes, and the simulation omits flight time, charging time, weather, no-fly zones, and collision avoidance.
  \item \textbf{Routing evidence.} The hard 0.70 filter can change a route after a small score change. The synthetic test checks multi-stop routing, but no WEPAStacks interval contains more than one active warehouse.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Recommended Refinements}

The first improvement is to distinguish an active dock with no arrivals from a dock that is inactive or not recorded. A \texttt{dock\_active} field and an operating calendar would help. Real queue lengths, service completions, and confirmed capacities would also allow the simulated target in Eq.~\eqref{eq:target} to be compared with real congestion.

Future evaluation should keep chronological data splits and report the number of samples behind each metric. The model should also be evaluated only on cases with current fill below 0.80. This would test whether it predicts new congestion instead of only detecting warehouses that are already full. Any new calibration method should be fitted on validation data and tested on a new untouched period.

The routing threshold should be compared on a validation simulation before one value is selected. Future policies could also require a minimum pickup load. The ACO penalty could be changed to include expected overflow, although this would make the algorithm more complex.

Future operational routing tests need real or realistic time-series scenarios with several active warehouses at the same time. The completed synthetic benchmark should then be extended to larger networks where exact search becomes difficult. Running more seeds on the current one-active-warehouse stream would not provide new route-order evidence.

\section{Conclusion}

The final system has two stages. First, the MLP produces one risk score for each warehouse, and a fixed 0.70 threshold selects non-empty warehouses. Second, single-drone ACO builds a distance-based route over the selected set. The route objective penalises unvisited active warehouses before distance, and the payload is shared in proportion to the available goods.

For Warehouse~1 and the simulated target, the MLP produced strong and stable prediction results. The 0.70 MLP--ACO policy reduced overflow by 11.38\% relative to current-state routing, although it used more distance and energy. All checked constraints were satisfied. ACO also matched exact search in all 150 synthetic runs, but exact search was faster at this small scale. Warehouses~2--4 still have no positive test cases, and the operational stream does not test multi-stop routing. The results therefore support prediction, pickup filtering, and basic ACO correctness, but not a real multi-stop routing benefit.

\section{Metric Definitions}

For true positives $TP$, false positives $FP$, true negatives $TN$, and false negatives $FN$:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, &
\mathrm{Recall} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FN},\\
\mathrm{Specificity} &= \frac{TN}{TN+FP}, &
\mathrm{F1} &= \frac{2\,\mathrm{Precision}\,\mathrm{Recall}}
{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}},\\
\mathrm{Balanced\ Accuracy} &= \frac{\mathrm{Recall}+\mathrm{Specificity}}{2}.
\end{align}
The Brier score is the mean squared difference between a predicted score and the binary target. The held-out value is 0.0122. The accompanying reliability plot shows weak support and deviations in middle-score bins, so this value alone is not treated as proof of full calibration.
